# Lenses and Focal Length

Focal length shapes how an audience relates to a subject. It is one of the most expressive choices a cinematographer makes.

## Wide lenses (14mm–35mm)
Wide lenses exaggerate depth and distance. Space between foreground and background feels stretched, and objects close to the lens loom large. Wide lenses make environments feel expansive or overwhelming and are useful for establishing geography. When pushed close to a face they distort features, creating unease, alienation, or comic grotesqueness. Handheld work on wide lenses reads as raw and immediate.

## Normal lenses (40mm–58mm)
A "normal" lens (around 50mm on full frame) approximates human perspective. It is neutral and unobtrusive, drawing little attention to itself. Directors who want the audience to forget the camera favor normal lenses for naturalistic drama.

## Long lenses (85mm–200mm+)
Long lenses compress space, flattening the distance between subject and background. They isolate a subject against a soft, blurred background (shallow depth of field), pulling the viewer's attention to the face. Telephoto compression makes approaching figures seem to make no progress, useful for dread or futility. Long lenses also let the camera observe from a distance, creating a voyeuristic or detached feeling.

## Depth of field
Wide apertures and long lenses produce shallow depth of field, isolating the subject. Deep focus keeps foreground and background sharp, letting the eye roam and holding multiple planes of action in tension.
