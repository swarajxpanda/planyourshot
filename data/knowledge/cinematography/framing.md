# Framing

Framing is the decision of what to include, what to exclude, and where to place the subject within the rectangle.

## Headroom and lead room
Comfortable headroom feels balanced; cramped headroom feels tense or trapped; excessive headroom isolates a small figure in a large space. Lead room (space in the direction a subject looks or moves) feels natural; removing it creates unease and confinement.

## Restricted and open framing
Restricted framing hides information — a subject pressed to the edge, a doorway cropping the view, negative space where a threat might appear. It withholds and generates suspense. Open framing shows everything and feels safe, honest, or exposed.

## Balance and imbalance
A centered, symmetrical frame feels formal, stable, or confrontational. An off-balance frame with weight on one side feels unstable and unresolved, keeping the viewer subconsciously alert.

## Frames within frames
Doorways, windows, mirrors, and architecture can frame a subject inside the frame. This isolates them, implies entrapment or surveillance, or separates them psychologically from others in the scene.

## Negative space
Large empty areas around a subject convey loneliness, vulnerability, or scale. Placing a character small in a wide, empty frame emphasizes isolation. Filling the frame tightly creates intimacy or claustrophobia.
