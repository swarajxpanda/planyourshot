# Lighting

Lighting sets mood, directs the eye, and models three-dimensional form. Its two biggest variables are contrast and direction.

## High-key vs low-key
High-key lighting is bright and even with few shadows. It feels safe, cheerful, clean, or clinical — common in comedy and commercials. Low-key lighting is dominated by shadow with pools of light and high contrast. It feels dramatic, mysterious, dangerous, or intimate — the language of noir, thriller, and horror.

## Direction of light
Front lighting flattens and reveals, minimizing texture. Side lighting models form and reveals texture, splitting a face into light and shadow for moral or psychological duality. Backlight separates a subject from the background and can create silhouette or a halo. Underlighting (from below) is unnatural and unsettling. Top light can feel oppressive or sculptural.

## Motivated and practical light
Light that appears to come from a visible source in the scene (a lamp, a window, a phone screen) is "motivated" and feels believable. Practical sources on camera — a desk lamp, a screen, a neon sign — ground the image and can become the key light, tying mood to the world of the scene.

## Color temperature
Warm light (tungsten, candle, sunset) feels intimate, nostalgic, or comforting. Cool light (moonlight, screen glow, fluorescent) feels lonely, sterile, or tense. Contrasting warm and cool within a frame creates visual and emotional tension.

## Silhouette
Lighting a subject from behind so they read as a dark shape conceals identity, creates threat, or emphasizes a figure's entrance.
