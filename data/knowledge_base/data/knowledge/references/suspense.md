# Shooting for Suspense

Suspense is built on anticipation, restricted information, and the audience's fear of what might happen.

## Core principles
Suspense comes from withholding. The camera shows just enough to make the audience anxious about what it isn't showing. Give the audience information a character lacks, or deny both audience and character alike, and let dread accumulate.

## Framing and composition
Use restricted framing and negative space where a threat could appear. Frame a subject small and off-center to keep them vulnerable and the frame unbalanced. Frames within frames (doorways, windows) imply watching or entrapment. Leave dangerous offscreen space just outside the edge.

## Camera movement
Slow, deliberate movement builds unease. A slow push-in on a character or a door tightens tension incrementally. A creeping, motivated move implies an unseen presence. Holding a static frame while the audience waits for something to enter is excruciating in the best way.

## Lensing and focus
Long lenses compress space and isolate the subject in shallow focus, so danger can lurk in the soft background. A rack focus that shifts attention to a newly-important element controls the timing of a reveal.

## Lighting
Low-key lighting with deep shadows conceals and threatens. Pools of light surrounded by darkness suggest safety is fragile and temporary. Silhouette an approaching figure to make an entrance ominous.

## Editing rhythm
Hold shots longer than comfortable to stretch tension, then cut sharply on the payoff. Cross-cutting between a threat and an unaware victim tightens the noose.
