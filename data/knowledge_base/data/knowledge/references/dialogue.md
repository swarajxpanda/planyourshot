# Shooting Dialogue Scenes

Dialogue scenes are about relationships, power, and shifting emotion — carried by how characters are framed relative to one another.

## Core principles
Coverage should serve the subtext, not just record the words. Who dominates the frame, who looks at whom, and when the camera moves closer all encode the emotional dynamics beneath the lines.

## Standard coverage
The classic pattern establishes the space with a two-shot or wide master, then covers each speaker in singles (often over-the-shoulder shots) for the exchange. The two-shot shows the relationship; the singles let the audience read each face in reaction.

## Over-the-shoulder and eyelines
Over-the-shoulder shots include the back of the listener's head, anchoring the speakers in shared space and preserving their spatial relationship. Consistent eyelines across the cut (the 180-degree rule) keep the conversation legible. Matching or mismatching the size of each character's singles encodes equality or imbalance of power.

## Framing for power and emotion
The character who dominates a scene can be given more frame, a lower angle, or a larger single; the weaker character can be framed higher, smaller, or more distant. As emotion rises, push in or cut to tighter singles; as characters connect or separate, adjust how much they share the frame.

## Camera movement
A slow push-in during a confession or realization intensifies the moment. Reframing as characters shift position keeps blocking alive. Static coverage keeps focus on performance.

## Reaction and the unspoken
Cutting to the listener rather than the speaker often reveals more than the line itself. The reaction shot is where dialogue scenes deliver their subtext.
